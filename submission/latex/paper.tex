\begin{abstract}
Physics-inspired graph neural networks (PI-GNNs) colour a graph by training a
fresh network to minimise a Potts relaxation of the conflict count, then
rounding its soft output. Credit for a good colouring could go to three bundled
ingredients: the neural optimiser, the information in its soft output, or the
decoding and repair applied afterwards. We separate them with controlled
interventions on 68 DIMACS graphs with one colour fewer than the chromatic
number. We find that (i)~training approaches discrete local minima but rarely
reaches them: only $42\%$ of runs are single-flip optimal after $10^5$ steps,
and wrong vertices are as confident as right ones, which an exact
softmax-gradient identity explains; (ii)~the network is not what makes PI-GNN
work: optimising one logit vector per vertex gives better raw colourings,
PI-GNN's modest advantage after repair vanishes without dropout, and directly
noise-injected logits beat PI-GNN on $44$ of $68$ graphs; (iii)~the learned
probabilities carry real coordinated information, yet a graph-only decoder
given no learned information does as well overall. The optimiser, not the
architecture or the decoder, decides the outcome.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A $k$-colouring of $G=(V,E)$ gives every vertex one of $k$ colours; an edge
whose endpoints share a colour is a \emph{conflict}, and the chromatic number
$\chi(G)$ is the fewest colours allowing none. Minimising conflicts is NP-hard,
and unsupervised graph neural networks are a recent line of attack.
The physics-inspired GNN (PI-GNN) of \citet{schuetz2022colouring} trains, for
each graph, a new network whose softmax output is a \emph{soft colouring} $P$,
minimises a Potts energy equal to the expected number of conflicts, and rounds
$P$ to a hard colouring.

When such a pipeline produces a good colouring, \emph{what deserves the
credit}? Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows three ingredients that are always
evaluated together: (1)~the neural parameterisation and its optimiser,
(2)~the information in the soft output $P$, and (3)~the decoder and any local
repair. Critiques showing that greedy algorithms match or beat PI-GNNs
\cite{angelini2023cautionary,boettcher2023inability} tell us \emph{that} the
pipeline can fall short, not \emph{which} part is responsible. We ask:

\begin{quote}\itshape
Which ingredient of PI-GNN is responsible for the quality of its colourings:
the GNN optimiser, the learned soft output, or the decoding and repair?
\end{quote}

We split it into three sub-questions, each answered by intervening on one
ingredient while holding the others fixed (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}).
\textbf{RQ1} asks what training actually produces: does the decoded output
approach a discrete local minimum, and how good is that minimum?
\textbf{RQ2} asks whether the GNN is needed: does it beat optimising one free
logit vector per vertex on the same loss? \textbf{RQ3} asks whether the
learned $P$ helps a stronger decoder more than uninformative or corrupted
inputs do.

All experiments use $k=\chi(G)-1$ colours. Zero conflicts are then
impossible, so methods are compared on a graded conflict count rather than a
binary success rate that would saturate on easy graphs. Our contribution is a
controlled decomposition protocol (identical starting outputs, separately tuned
competitors, matched steps \emph{and} time, one shared repair, graph-level
paired statistics) and the answer it gives: training makes outputs confident
long before it makes them locally optimal; the network's edge over free logits
is small, depends on dropout, and is overturned by plain noise; and the learned
$P$ beats corrupted inputs but not a graph-only decoder.

\section{Background and Related Work}

\paragraph{Learning for combinatorial optimisation.} Learned methods either
construct solutions or guide classical solvers \cite{bengio2021ml4co}; for
colouring, \citet{lemos2019gcp} trained a supervised GNN to decide
$k$-colourability. Unsupervised approaches instead minimise a differentiable
relaxation per instance. \citet{karalias2020erdos} relax the objective to an
expectation under independent vertex distributions and recover a solution by
derandomised rounding \cite{raghavan1988derandomization}, with a guarantee in
terms of the relaxed loss. PI-GNN \cite{schuetz2022combopt,schuetz2022colouring}
applies the same idea with a QUBO or Potts energy \cite{wu1982potts} and a
GraphSAGE network \cite{hamilton2017sage}, reporting results competitive with
classical solvers on DIMACS benchmarks \cite{johnson1996dimacs}.

\paragraph{Critiques and ablations.} \citet{angelini2023cautionary} and
\citet{boettcher2023inability} showed that simple greedy algorithms match or
beat PI-GNN on maximum independent set and max-cut. These are end-to-end
comparisons: they show the pipeline can lose, not which component fails or why.
\citet{vanderbush2026nar} include a direct-logit (depth-zero) ablation of a
modified PI-GNN for colouring. We make that comparison the central object and
control what an ablation alone leaves open: a shared starting point, separate
tuning, matched time, noise controls and a common repair step. To our
knowledge, no study has isolated the contribution of each PI-GNN ingredient.

\paragraph{Classical references and training dynamics.} DSATUR
\cite{brelaz1979} greedily colours the most constrained vertex first. TabuCol
\cite{hertz1987tabu} is a local search that accepts worsening moves and
forbids recent reversals, and underlies state-of-the-art hybrids
\cite{galinier1999hybrid}; we use both as reference points. A network moves
its output through shared parameters, so one step moves many vertices at once
\cite{jacot2018ntk}, and dropout \cite{srivastava2014dropout} injects noise
into training; both matter for RQ2.

\section{Problem, Tools and Protocol}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Relaxation.} A soft colouring $P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}$ has a
probability row $p_v$ per vertex, obtained from logits by
$P=\mathrm{softmax}(Z)$. The Potts loss and hard conflict count are
\begin{align*}
L(P)&=\textstyle\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}p_u^{\top}p_v,\\
C(c)&=\textstyle\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}\mathbf{1}[c_u=c_v].
\end{align*}
$p_u^\top p_v$ is the probability that $u$ and $v$ draw the same colour, so
$L(P)$ is the expected conflict count when each vertex samples independently,
and $L=C$ when every row is one-hot. PI-GNN decodes by argmax.

\paragraph{Local optimality and repair.} Let $M_v(a)$ be the number of
neighbours of $v$ with colour $a$. Recolouring $v$ can remove at most
$\gamma_v=M_v(c_v)-\min_a M_v(a)$ conflicts; a colouring is \emph{single-flip
optimal} (SFO) if $\gamma_v=0$ for all $v$. Our shared repair, \emph{strict
descent}, repeatedly applies a move with the largest positive $\gamma_v$ and
stops at an SFO colouring. We report raw conflicts $C$ (how good a method's own
answer is) and $\Cd$ after descent (how good a \emph{starting point} it is);
in RQ2 these disagree.

\paragraph{Two facts frame the results:} what an ideal endpoint looks like,
and why training may not reach it.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:strict}
If $P^*$ is a strict local minimum of $L$ over all row-stochastic matrices,
every row is one-hot and every single-vertex recolouring strictly increases $C$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
With other rows fixed, $L=B+p_v^\top q_v$ where $q_v=\sum_{u\in N(v)}p_u$ is
affine in $p_v$. Moving mass $\epsilon$ between two colours $a,b$ in the
support of $p_v$ changes $L$ by $\pm\epsilon(q_v(a)-q_v(b))$, so one of the
two moves does not increase $L$, contradicting strictness. At a one-hot row,
moving towards colour $a$ changes $L$ by $t\,[M_v(a)-M_v(c_v)]$, which must be
positive.
\end{proof}

\noindent Training, however, moves logits, and for the Potts loss
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_{v,a}}=p_v(a)\bigl[q_v(a)-p_v^\top q_v\bigr].
\label{eq:grad}
\end{equation}
If $p_v$ is nearly one-hot on colour $c$, then for $a\neq c$ the factor
$p_v(a)\approx e^{-\Delta}$ is tiny, where $\Delta$ is the gap between the top
two logits, and for $a=c$ the bracket is $\approx q_v(c)-q_v(c)=0$. All logit
gradients of a \emph{saturated} vertex vanish, even when another colour would
remove many conflicts.

\paragraph{PI-GNN and benchmark.} We reimplement PI-GNN in PyTorch: trainable
Gaussian vertex embeddings (dimension 85), two mean-aggregation GraphSAGE
layers (hidden width 30, ReLU, dropout $0.47$), trained full-batch with AdamW
\cite{loshchilov2019adamw}. We use the 68 DIMACS graphs whose chromatic number
is proven \cite{held2012safe,gualandi2012exact}, with 11 to 1{,}000 vertices
from 14 families (queen, Mycielski, register allocation, Leighton, flat,
DSJC/DSJR random, and others), each with 5 seeds for $10^5$ steps, no early
stopping, and dropout off at evaluation. Every model type is tuned separately
by the same two-stage random search on 13 disjoint synthetic graphs, then
frozen.

\paragraph{Comparison rule.} The graph is the unit of analysis: we average the
5 seeds per graph, then compare paired graph means $X_g,Y_g$ through
$d_g=(X_g-Y_g)/\max(Y_g,1)$. A graph is a practical tie if
$|X_g-Y_g|<1$ conflict or $|d_g|\le 5\%$; otherwise a win or loss. We report
wins/ties/losses (W/T/L), the median $d_g$, and a two-sided Wilcoxon
signed-rank test \cite{wilcoxon1945}. A directional claim requires a small $p$
(Bonferroni-corrected over the primary comparisons of RQ1 and RQ2), a median
beyond $5\%$, and more wins than losses; anything else is ``not
distinguishable'', which is not a claim of equality. One rule serves all
three RQs; it was fixed before the main RQ2 results were computed. The per-graph rule is primary because
conflict counts span four orders of magnitude, so a mean is dominated by a few
dense graphs.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{RQ1: What does training produce?}

Figure~\ref{fig:rq1} tracks the argmax output over training. The fraction of
SFO runs rises from $0\%$ to $42\%$ ($143/340$), and the median share of
conflicts that descent can still remove falls from $95\%$ to $10\%$. Both
curves flatten: from $5{\cdot}10^4$ to $10^5$ steps the remaining gain does not
move. Extending 13 still-improving graphs to $3{\cdot}10^5$ steps improves
conflicts after descent on 12, yet SFO runs go from $0/65$ to only $1/65$.
\emph{Longer training finds better basins without reaching their bottoms.}

Usually only a few vertices are the problem (median $2.5$ improvable vertices
per run, under $1\%$ of the graph), and those vertices are \emph{confident}.
In the median graph, $99.7\%$ of vertices have $\max_a p_v(a)\ge 0.99$, and so
do $100\%$ of the improvable ones; their median top-two logit gap is about
$150$, so $p_v(a)\approx e^{-150}$ for the better colour. By
Eq.~\eqref{eq:grad} such a vertex gets almost no gradient towards the better
colour: a plausible mechanism for frozen errors, though not a proven cause,
since shared parameters can still move it indirectly.

Is the endpoint at least good? Table~\ref{tab:rq1} compares PI-GNN after
descent with references given the same descent. Training clearly beats random
and epoch-1 starts, but it is not distinguishable from the 1979 DSATUR
heuristic, and a time-matched TabuCol finds fewer conflicts on 41 graphs. The
queen family makes the point sharply: $85\%$ of queen runs are already SFO,
yet TabuCol wins on all 12 queen graphs. A local minimum can be shallow.
Failure modes also differ by family: register-allocation runs are rarely SFO
but only a few conflicts from it, while some dense DSJR runs stall near the
uniform distribution, a stationary point of $L$.

\subsection{RQ2: Is the network needed?}

The graph enters the Potts loss, so a network is not needed to ``use'' it. The
natural network-free competitor, arm B, optimises the logit matrix $Z$
directly, starting from PI-GNN's exact initial output so that only the
optimisation differs. Other arms each change one thing (Table~\ref{tab:rq2}),
including Gaussian noise of scale $\sigma$ added to $Z$ during training
(B-noise) and PI-GNN linearised at initialisation, $Z=Z_0+J_0\delta$ with a
frozen Jacobian (D), which tests whether the network acts as a fixed
graph-dependent preconditioner. B-noise reuses B's tuning.

\paragraph{The verdict flips with repair.} Free logits give \emph{better raw}
colourings than PI-GNN (32 wins, 13 losses, $p=0.002$), but after descent
PI-GNN is better (26 wins, 17 losses, median $6\%$, $p<0.001$). Both readings
hold at matched wall-clock time, where B gets about $2.9\times$ more steps and
barely changes. The SFO column explains the flip: B ends single-flip optimal
in all 340 runs, so descent has nothing to add, while PI-GNN stops short in
$58\%$ of runs but in basins whose bottoms are slightly deeper. B still has
the lower \emph{mean} after descent (108.6 vs.\ 114.9): PI-GNN's large relative
wins come on low-conflict graphs, B's on high-conflict ones.

\paragraph{The advantage is noise, not architecture.} Removing dropout from
PI-GNN erases its edge: the result loses to PI-GNN on 21 graphs and is not
distinguishable from B. Adding noise to free logits overturns it: at
$\sigma=2$ they beat PI-GNN after descent on 44 graphs, losing on 3, and beat
plain B on 53 with no losses. A plausible reading is the one behind simulated
annealing \cite{kirkpatrick1983annealing}: persistent noise prevents early
commitment to the first basin. Indeed, at step 100, $69\%$ of PI-GNN's
vertices are already confident against $0.2\%$ for B.

\paragraph{Noise, but of the right kind.} Dropout and logit noise act in
opposite directions on confidence. The arms with dropout (A and C) end with
enormous logit gaps (204 and 139); without dropout the gap is 18, and with
logit noise it shrinks to 8. We interpret this as the network inflating its
weights to survive losing half its hidden units; with dropout off at
evaluation every unit votes at full strength and outputs saturate, freezing
the RQ1 errors (Eq.~\ref{eq:grad}). Logit noise gives exploration without
saturation, and the best arm is the \emph{least} confident one.

\paragraph{Aggregation helps only inside the neural recipe; a frozen network
fails.} The node MLP C loses to PI-GNN after descent on 28 graphs (5 wins), so
aggregation contributes \emph{within} the neural recipe, although a simpler
procedure does better without it. D is the worst arm (no wins against PI-GNN
or B), so PI-GNN's help is not a fixed preconditioner from initialisation. B
and B-rand are not distinguishable (14/42/12): the network's initial output
gives no useful head start.

\subsection{RQ3: Is it the soft output or the decoder?}

A decoder that reads the graph can colour well even from a meaningless $P$, so
we fix the decoder and vary its input. The decoder is derandomised
conditional expectation (D-CE) \cite{raghavan1988derandomization,karalias2020erdos}:
it fixes vertices one at a time in DSATUR order, giving $v$ the colour $a$
that minimises the number of fixed neighbours coloured $a$ plus $\sum p_u(a)$
over unfixed neighbours $u$. It guarantees $C\le L(P)$, and on exactly uniform
$P$ it reduces to DSATUR. A near-uniform input is thus a \emph{graph-only}
decoder with no learned information. We compare PI-GNN's trained $P$ with it
and with three corruptions: the untrained output, rows shuffled across
vertices, and colours permuted independently within each row, which keeps
every row's confidence but breaks agreement between neighbours.

\paragraph{Real information, but not more than the graph alone.}
Table~\ref{tab:rq3} shows that trained $P$ clearly beats every corruption,
including the colour-scrambled one that preserves confidence exactly. The
useful content is therefore \emph{coordination} between neighbours, not
confidence. Yet against the graph-only decoder the trained $P$ is not
distinguishable (median $0\%$, $p=0.23$), consistent with RQ1's tie against
DSATUR. Confidently wrong information is worse than none (near-uniform beats
scrambled on 41 graphs), so beating a corrupted input is a weak test. The
aggregate tie also hides a family split: trained $P$ wins on all 12 queen
graphs but loses to the graph-only decoder on DSJR, register-allocation and
miles graphs.

\paragraph{Most of the decoder's gain is repair.} D-CE improves raw argmax
substantially, but once argmax also gets descent the gain is $2.8\%$, inside
the tie band, though consistent and cheap. Best-of-32 sampling adds nothing
(2/65/1) at $26\times$ the cost: saturated rows make sampling reproduce argmax.

\paragraph{The optimiser still decides.} Decoding all nine RQ2 arms with D-CE
narrows the spread between them (median within-graph range down by $10\%$) but
preserves the ranking: B-noise-2 still beats PI-GNN (35/29/4), D stays last,
and B-noise-2 is the only arm whose $P$ clearly beats the graph-only decoder.
A better optimiser yields a better $P$, and that advantage survives decoding.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

Returning to Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, our answer to the research question is
that \textbf{the optimiser decides the outcome, but the GNN is not what makes
it good}. Differences between optimisers survive every decoder we tried, while
PI-GNN's specific ingredients contribute little, and the strongest procedure
we found has no network at all.

This sharpens the end-to-end critiques
\cite{angelini2023cautionary,boettcher2023inability}. PI-GNN is not weak
because it fails to learn: its $P$ carries real coordinated structure
(Table~\ref{tab:rq3}). It is weak because its optimisation saturates, leaving
confident but improvable vertices (Eq.~\ref{eq:grad}), and because what helps
it, noise, is available more cheaply without a network.

For evaluating such methods we suggest: report raw \emph{and} repaired
quality, which can rank methods oppositely; use free logits on the same loss
as the network-free baseline; test learned outputs against an uninformative
input under the same decoder, not only corrupted ones; never treat confidence
as correctness; and report per-family results.

\paragraph{Limitations.} One architecture, one regime ($k=\chi-1$) and 68
heterogeneous graphs limit generality. $\sigma=2$ was the best of three values
on the test graphs, not tuned on the synthetic set, and was not compared with
TabuCol. Logit noise and dropout differ, so the noise account is plausible
rather than isolated; saturation rests on evaluation-time diagnostics, not a
training intervention; D is one linearisation, not every preconditioner. Time
matching used shared mixed-GPU jobs, and our p-values are descriptive, with no
confidence intervals.

\section{Conclusion}

Intervening on one ingredient at a time shows that PI-GNN's outputs become
confident long before they become locally optimal, that the network's edge
over plain logit optimisation is small and exceeded by simple noise, and that
its learned probabilities are worth about as much as the graph itself given to
a greedy decoder. Confidence, local optimality and quality are distinct, and
conflating them overstates what the network contributes. A natural next step
is to tune noisy logit optimisation and compare it with strong local search.
